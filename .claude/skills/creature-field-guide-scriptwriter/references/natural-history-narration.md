# Natural-history narration

## What to borrow from the genre

High-end natural-history documentaries commonly build emotional investment by following a particular animal through a problem, while the narrator moves between intimate observation and wider biological meaning. The visual event comes first; explanation follows it. The narration should make a behavior legible without pretending the camera can read an animal's mind.

## Sentence patterns

Use patterns like:

- “For most of the afternoon, K7 remains beneath the same patch of light. It looks like idleness, until the bulb on its back begins to warm and swell.”
- “The record says the seed is present from birth. It does not say who planted it. That distinction matters, because K7 treats the thing on its back less like a burden than a second organ.”
- “I cannot tell whether the two are cooperating. I can only see that, when one moves into shadow, the other follows.”

Avoid:

- “K7 sleeps. K7 eats. K7 evolves.”
- “This is amazing. This is dangerous. This is evolution.”
- A paragraph that explains a fact before showing the behavior that makes the fact relevant.

## Documentary restraint

Do not over-anthropomorphize. “It waits”, “it avoids”, and “it returns” are observations. “It feels lonely” or “it wants revenge” requires evidence and should normally be replaced by a visible behavior or qualified as interpretation.

## Transformation scenes

Do not invent biological events merely because they look cinematic. For a Bulbasaur-to-Ivysaur transition, use swelling, weight shift, growth, light, silhouette, and the flower bud opening; do not use snake-like shedding unless the creature bible explicitly establishes moulting.
