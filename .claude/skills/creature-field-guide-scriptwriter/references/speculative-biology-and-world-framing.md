# Speculative biology and world framing

## Ability worksheet

For every supernatural-looking ability, answer:

1. What physical structure produces it?
2. Where is energy or material stored?
3. What triggers release?
4. What does the output do to the environment?
5. What does it cost the animal?
6. What happens when the mechanism fails or is overused?

The final narration should reveal these answers through behavior, not dump them as a textbook list.

## Food-web worksheet

Map the subject's place in the habitat:

- food and water sources;
- predators and defense;
- competitors for the same resource;
- parasites, symbionts, or mutualists;
- seasonal bottleneck;
- reproduction and juvenile survival;
- effect on neighboring species.

Do not add a predator only to manufacture drama. A drought, lack of light, disease, territorial pressure, or a failed migration can carry the same narrative weight.

## In-world narrator

The narrator should have a believable reason to know what is being described. A field assistant may use a logbook and measurements; a ranger may know tracks and seasons; a magizoologist may use concealment and local records. Keep this framing in the story bible and use it to shape what the narrator can and cannot know.

**This channel's narrator is fixed: Dr. Holth.** A field biologist from an Earth-like world, searching
for creatures in which life and energy cannot be separated, who observes and never intervenes. Read
`docs/NARRATOR.md` before writing a single line. Two hard rules: he never speaks his own name, and the
word "Gilbert" never appears anywhere — his full name is an anagram of the twist in the owner's game.

## Deadpan sincerity

Describe extraordinary abilities with calm precision. The comedy, if any, comes from the gap between the absurd creature and the seriousness of the explanation, never from insulting the subject.
