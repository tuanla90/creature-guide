# Creature Field Guide Scriptwriter

Write the episode as a narrated natural-history film, not as a list of Pokedex facts. Follow one observable subject through a meaningful period, and let facts arrive because the subject does something.

## Non-negotiable narrative rules

- Every episode needs a concrete survival question, environmental pressure, and a limit or deadline. The pressure may be drought, migration, injury, scarce light, reproduction, habitat change, social competition, or transformation; it does not need to be a predator attack.
- Build a three-part emotional arc: setup (what the subject needs), confrontation (what blocks it), and resolution (what changes, adapts, or remains unresolved).
- Start with an unanswered observation, not a definition.
- Use a real subject identifier (`K7`, `B-02`, `female 3`) unless the story world has a credible reason for a local name.
- Do not invent a cute nickname merely to create emotion. If a nickname is used, explain who gave it and why.
- Separate canon record, direct observation, Earth comparison, and hypothesis. Never present an inference as fact.
- Use complete spoken sentences with natural connective tissue. Avoid strings of fragments such as "Silver eyes. Cape flying. She turns."
- Let each paragraph contain a small movement: observation -> interpretation -> consequence or question.
- Prefer concrete verbs and sensory detail over abstract labels.
- Keep the narrator curious and fallible. They may revise an earlier belief after new evidence.
- Use one central biological question per episode and return to it at the end.
- Treat predators and competitors as ecological forces, not villains. Do not force a moral judgment onto survival behavior.
- Rotate themes across episodes: predation, symbiosis, competition, adaptation, reproduction, aging, migration, specialization, habitat loss, and transformation.
- Treat fictional abilities as speculative biology: propose anatomy, energy flow, chemistry, physics, cost, and failure modes rather than calling an ability magic. Label invented mechanisms as hypotheses or in-world models.
- Build an in-world food web when the subject's diet or predators are relevant. Explain who eats whom, what resource limits the population, and which relationships are predation, competition, parasitism, or mutualism.
- Use a deadpan documentary tone even when the creature is visually absurd. Do not mock weak or comic creatures; find the viable strategy behind the trait.

## Voice and rhythm

Write for Vietnamese voiceover, not captions. Vary sentence length: a 12–20 word observation, followed by a longer reflective sentence, then a short landing line. Use commas and conjunctions to create breath, but do not add artificial line breaks between every clause.

Each beat should have 2–4 spoken paragraphs, not isolated slogans. A beat normally follows:

`what the camera sees -> what the narrator notices -> what it might mean -> what remains unknown`

Use quiet pauses only after an image or a completed thought. Avoid constant rhetorical questions; reserve them for the episode's central mystery or a real turning point.

Do not fill the entire soundtrack with speech. Plan room for breath, wind, water, footsteps, calls, impact sounds, and music. Target roughly 15–25% non-verbal space unless the scene is intentionally information-dense. Use shorter sentences only during pursuit, danger, or rapid action; use longer connected sentences for observation and reflection.

## Episode architecture

1. Cold open: a strange behavior or unanswered physical clue.
2. Subject and place: identify the individual and establish the field conditions.
3. Daily survival: food, energy, shelter, sleep.
4. Social life: spacing, cooperation, conflict, communication.
5. Threat: a predator, weather event, scarcity, injury, or competition.
6. Biological interpretation: connect the observation to a real-world analogue, clearly marked as comparison or hypothesis.
7. Change over time: season, growth, reproduction, or transformation.
8. Payoff: answer or refine the opening question.
9. Handoff: end with a specific unresolved clue leading to the next creature.

## Naming and continuity

Keep the subject ID stable across narration, captions, filenames, and prompts. If several animals are shown, label them by observable features (`female 2`, `the juvenile with the torn ear`) rather than pretending they are the same named subject. Never use a composite of multiple creatures as one individual without labeling it as a reconstruction.

Use three separate identity layers: species label, field identifier, and optional narrative name. Assign a narrative name only when the world, local community, or research team has a credible reason to use it, and only after the visual marker is locked. A scar-based name is valid only if the scar remains consistent across the reference sheet and every shot.

Choose an archetype as a planning aid, not as a personality shortcut: Matriarch, Challenger, Underdog, Survivor, Specialist, Symbiont, Wanderer, Caregiver, Relic, or Misfit. The archetype must be demonstrated through visible behavior.

## In-world researcher and taxonomy

Choose a narrator who belongs to the subject's world when that strengthens immersion: a field assistant, magizoologist, ranger, or local natural philosopher. Do not mention games, CGI, filming, prompts, or production tools inside the fictional narration. Production notes may still use those terms outside the voiceover.

Scientific-style names are optional and must be marked as proposed taxonomy, not official canon. Use a plausible binomial only when it adds clarity; do not invent Latin names for every creature. Keep the familiar name, proposed scientific name, field identifier, and local name in separate fields.

## Speculative biology guardrails

- Every fictional mechanism needs a source, storage organ or tissue, trigger, output, metabolic cost, and failure mode.
- Earth analogies are explanatory models, not proof that the fictional creature has the same anatomy.
- Do not universalize metamorphosis. Some species may molt, some may undergo gradual growth, some may reorganize bone and muscle, and some may have a staged life cycle. Choose the mechanism from the creature bible and visual evidence.
- Avoid pseudo-precision. A precise voltage, temperature, or Latin name should have an in-world measurement or be framed as an estimate.
- Show consequences: heat, exhaustion, hunger, tissue strain, recovery time, habitat dependence, or predation risk.

## Output contract

Return: episode premise, central question, subject/identifier, evidence ledger, beat outline, full narration, visual direction per beat, pronunciation map for VBee, and an accuracy/uncertainty pass. The visual plan must never imply that a generated image proves a biological fact.
